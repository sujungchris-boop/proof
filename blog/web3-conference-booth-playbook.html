The Web3 Conference Booth Playbook

By PROOF · Seoul · August 2026 · 8 min read

A conference floor is the most competitive real estate in Web3: hundreds of stands, thousands of people walking past at speed, and everyone shouting the same three words. Here's how to build, staff and run a booth that people actually stop at — and what to do with the conversations afterwards.

Most booth budgets are decided before anyone has asked what the booth is for. The sponsorship tier gets bought, a build gets ordered to fill the footprint, four people fly in, and the plan for the two days is "be there." Two days later the team goes home with a spreadsheet of badge scans and a vague sense that it went fine.

A booth is a good tool — the best one available for reach. But reach only pays off if the stand earns a stop, the stop turns into a conversation, and the conversation survives the flight home. Those are three separate design problems, and each one has a boring, solvable answer.

Before you build: decide what the booth is for

Write down the single outcome that makes the spend worth it. In practice it's almost always one of four: launch awareness (people should leave knowing what you are), pipeline (BD conversations with a specific profile), recruiting, or ecosystem credibility (being visibly present next to the projects you want to be grouped with).

These build different booths. An awareness booth needs a headline visible from thirty feet and something photographable. A pipeline booth needs seating, quiet, and staff who can qualify. A recruiting booth needs engineers on the stand, not marketers. If you can't name the outcome, the format may be wrong in the first place — our guide to choosing between a booth, side event and dinner is the step before this one.

Sizing, location and what the floor plan is really telling you

Booth packages are sold by square meters, but what you're actually buying is traffic quality. Before you pick a spot, ask the organiser for the floor plan and look for four things: the main entrance flow, where the food and coffee sit, where the stages are, and where the sponsor tiers cluster.

  • Near the entrance — high volume, low intent. People are still orienting and haven't decided who they came to see.
  • Near coffee, food or seating — reliably the best traffic on most floors. People are stationary, unhurried and looking for something to look at.
  • Near a stage — feast and famine. Dead during sessions, flooded in the ten minutes after one ends. Great if you can staff the surges.
  • Back corners and side aisles — cheap for a reason. Only workable if you have a genuine destination reason for people to walk there.

On size: a compact stand run well beats a large stand run thin, every time. A 3×3 with two sharp people and one working demo will out-perform a 6×6 with a big logo and nobody who can answer a technical question.

The build: one idea, readable at speed

The single most common booth failure is trying to say five things. A person walking past gives you roughly three seconds and about ten words of attention. Everything else in the build is for after they've stopped.

What a working booth actually needs:

  • A headline you can read from across the aisle. Big, high-contrast, plain-language. Not your tagline — what you do. "Cross-chain settlement for game studios" beats "Redefining the future of value."
  • One thing to do or watch. A live product demo on a screen, a working device, a physical interaction. Motion catches eyes that static graphics don't.
  • Somewhere to talk. Even two stools and a ledge. If every conversation happens standing in the aisle, they all end at ninety seconds.
  • Storage. Bags, coats, spare merch and empty boxes have to go somewhere that isn't the visible floor. Booths get ugly by hour four without it.
  • Power and connectivity you've tested. Order more power drops than you think you need, bring your own extension leads and adapters, and never assume venue Wi-Fi will hold. A demo that fails at a busy moment is worse than no demo.
The three-second test: stand ten meters away from your own render and read it as a stranger would. If you can't say what the company does out loud in one sentence, the build isn't finished — no amount of staffing will fix it on the day.

Merch that isn't landfill

Giveaways work when they solve a conference problem: charging cables, power banks, tote bags, decent water bottles, good coffee. They fail when they're another branded stress ball. If you're shipping merch internationally, factor in customs and lead time — this is one of the most common reasons a booth opens incomplete. And be careful with anything that draws a queue of people who only want the free thing: high footfall with zero intent looks great in photos and does nothing for pipeline.

Staffing: the part that decides everything

You can recover from a mediocre build with excellent staffing. You cannot recover from bad staffing with any build. The rules are simple and almost universally broken:

  • Never fewer than two on the stand, never all four at once. One person alone can't step away or handle two visitors; four standing in a huddle is a closed door.
  • Rotate every two hours. Floor shifts are exhausting. Build a written rota with breaks, meals and clear ownership of the demo.
  • Nobody sits behind the table looking at a laptop. Stand at the front edge of the booth, open posture, facing the aisle.
  • Have a technical person on-stand at peak hours. In Web3 the good questions are technical, and "I'll connect you with our engineer" loses the moment.
  • Agree the opener. Not "how are you doing today?" — a specific question tied to your headline: "Are you building on the game side or the infra side?" It qualifies and starts a real conversation in one line.
  • Brief the whole team on the one-sentence pitch, the demo, and the disqualifiers — who you're not for. Knowing when to end a conversation politely is a skill, and it protects the hours that matter.

If you're staffing a booth in a market where you don't speak the language, add a bilingual host who can hold a real conversation, not just translate. In Korea especially, that's often the difference between a polite exchange and an actual meeting — one of several reasons we treat local presence as its own workstream in the Korea market-entry playbook.

Standing out without shouting

Every booth on the floor has a screen, a logo wall and a stack of stickers. Differentiation comes from doing one thing the others don't:

  • Show the product working. A live transaction, a real dashboard, an actual device. Astonishingly rare, and it's the single most effective thing on any Web3 floor.
  • Put a human specialist on the stand at a fixed time. "Our protocol lead is here 2–4pm" turns a booth into a destination and gives people a reason to come back.
  • Own a sense other than sight. Good coffee, real seating, a quiet corner. On a loud floor, comfort is a differentiator.
  • Give the floor a reason to move. A short scheduled moment at the stand — a five-minute demo on the hour, a small giveaway draw — creates a crowd, and crowds create crowds.
  • Extend the booth off the floor. The booth generates reach; a dinner or side event that evening converts the best of it. That pairing is the highest-leverage structure we see, and it's the backbone of our TOKEN2049 operator's checklist.

Capture and follow-up

Badge scanners give you contact details and nothing else. Two weeks later nobody remembers which of the 300 scans was the one worth calling. Fix it at the booth: after each real conversation, log who they are, what they asked about, and the agreed next step, in a shared sheet or CRM, on the spot. Thirty seconds of typing while it's fresh is worth more than the entire scan list.

Then follow up inside about 48 hours, referencing the specific conversation, from the person who had it. Rank contacts before you send: the handful worth a real meeting get a personal note first; everyone else can wait for the general one. A booth doesn't produce results at the conference — it produces them in the two weeks after.

A booth is not a marketing asset you buy; it's an operation you run for two days. Decide the outcome, build for one idea, staff it properly, capture context, and follow up fast. Do those five things and a modest stand will beat the biggest logo on the floor — which, from where we sit, is the entire game.

FAQ

How many people do you need to staff a Web3 conference booth?

Plan for at least two people on the booth at all times, and enough headcount to rotate them off every couple of hours. A small booth typically needs three to four people across the day; a larger stand with a demo station needs more, plus someone whose only job is qualifying and routing conversations. Tired staff are the most common reason a good booth stops working after lunch.

What should you actually build on a Web3 conference booth?

Build for one idea, not five. The essentials are a headline readable from across the aisle, one thing a visitor can do or watch, somewhere to have a two-minute conversation without shouting, and power and connectivity you have tested. Screens full of dense slides and a table of leaflets are what most booths default to, and they are exactly what people walk past.

Is a conference booth worth it for a Web3 project?

A booth buys reach — broad visibility to everyone who walks the floor — which is worth it when you are launching, hiring, or trying to own a category. If your goal is twenty specific relationships, a curated side event or investor dinner usually returns more for the same money. Decide the single outcome first, then choose the format against it.

How do you follow up on conference booth leads?

Capture context, not just contact details — who they are, what they asked, and the agreed next step, logged at the booth while it is fresh. Send the first follow-up within about 48 hours, while the conference is still recent, and make it specific to that conversation. A generic blast to a scanned list is how most booth pipeline quietly dies.
